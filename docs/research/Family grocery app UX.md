# Let the family catalog learn itself

FamilyOS's groceries domain gets better through one structural change, not more screens: **every line anyone types should become (or reuse) a household product that learns its own details over time**. The leading list apps (OurGroceries, AnyList, Bring!, Apple Reminders) all work this way. Unknown text is never a dead end. It joins the household's own catalog, gets a guessed category, and comes back first in typeahead next time. Today FamilyOS splits the world into "products" and "free-text lines", and that split causes most of the friction the family has hit. The evidence supports the proposed mock's core ideas: one add box, a tap-to-fill item sheet, "How it comes" as a package-size field separate from "how many", and a Pantry cleanup queue with merge. Change three things. Replace a typed **"Aisle" field with per-store section order learned from corrections and check-off sequence**. Treat **"How many we usually get" as a smart default** on the list entry rather than a catalog chore. And keep the Pantry a **light cleanup inbox, not Grocy-style stock counting**, which users abandon. In-store mode is the other big lever. Tap-to-check with items sinking into "In the cart", undo instead of confirmation, a screen kept awake, offline queueing, a "heading to the store" push and thumb-reachable controls are all cheap on FamilyOS's existing PWA and Web Push stack. Open Food Facts stays the right barcode source. Its US coverage is partial, though, so what matters most is the household's own barcode-to-product memory on the "not found" path.

## Free text should feed the catalog, not bypass it

The most-praised entry pattern is a hybrid: a built-in dictionary merged with the household's own history. AnyList suggests from "our built-in database of grocery items, as well as items you have marked as favorites and items you've recently entered" ([AnyList Help](https://help.anylist.com/articles/feature-overview-adding-items/)). OurGroceries keeps a per-household master list of **every item ever entered**, matched by multi-word prefix ("pe bu" finds peanut butter), so "you'll rarely have to type more than the first letter or two" ([OurGroceries FAQ](https://www.ourgroceries.com/faq); [User Guide](https://www.ourgroceries.com/user-guide)). In Bring!, an item missing from the catalog becomes a custom item, and "the app assigns suitable icons and categories automatically" ([Bring! Help Center](https://www.getbring.com/help-center-main-categories/items-lists)). AnyList then carries stored properties such as price and photo forward from a matching favorite or recent item ([AnyList Release Notes Feb 2020](https://help.anylist.com/articles/release-notes-anylist-feb-2020/)). None of these apps has two classes of item. The free-text line *is* a catalog row that hasn't been filled in yet.

That is why FamilyOS's current Enter behaviour is right in spirit and wrong in data. Keeping Enter as "add what I typed" is correct: in NN/g testing, users picked an offered suggestion in **only 23% of cases** ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/site-search-suggestions/)), so forcing a choice would slow most adds. The fix is in storage. Enter should match on a normalized name (lowercase, trimmed, simple plural folding) and reuse the existing household product, or quietly create a new one marked "needs details". The mock's single add box (search, scan, again) fits this model. Its "needs details" queue is the natural place where those quietly created products surface later.

The typeahead itself should follow the published rules. Show **4 to 8 suggestions on mobile**, with no inner scrollbar. Style the predicted part differently from what the user typed. Support arrow keys and Enter on laptops ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/autocomplete-design)). Rank the household's own frequent and recent products above seed-dictionary matches. That mirrors OurGroceries' frequency sort and AnyList's recents, and it keeps suggestions relevant, which NN/g says matters because irrelevant suggestions "sidetrack users". If the typed name matches something **already on the list**, the suggestion should read "Already on list — add one more?" and bump the quantity rather than create a duplicate. Google Keep shipped duplicate detection on entry back in 2016 ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2016-06-01-google-keep-can-autocomplete-your-grocery-list-entries.html)). Kids benefit most from spelling-tolerant matching, since NN/g notes that error-prevention aids like autocorrect reduce children's frustration ([NN/g: Kids' cognition](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/kids-cognition/)).

Inline quantity parsing is worth adding, but leniently. Listonic accepts "tomatoes 500g" with g, kg, L, mL, oz, lb, gal and pt ([Listonic](https://listonic.com/add-details-to-your-shopping-list-items-pro)), and Bring! accepts "2 kg apples" ([Bring! Help Center](https://www.getbring.com/help-center-main-categories/items-lists)). NN/g's mobile-input guidance says to accept flexible formats and reformat internally ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-input-checklist/)). The parser should only peel a leading or trailing "number + known unit" or "x2". It should show the result as chips ("Chicken · 2 lb") and fall back to the whole string as the name when unsure. Neither vendor documents how it handles "7 Up", "2% milk" or "9-grain bread", so FamilyOS needs a guard list and tests for numbers that belong to the product name. Voice is not worth chasing through assistants. Amazon shut down the Alexa List Skill API on July 1, 2024, and Google banned third-party conversational actions in June 2023 ([AnyList](https://help.anylist.com/articles/alexa-skill-update-july-2024/); [OurGroceries FAQ](https://www.ourgroceries.com/faq)). If voice matters, use browser speech input feeding the same parser, or an iOS Shortcut that calls an authenticated endpoint.

## Two plain fields fix the "31.1 lb" problem that "Default unit" created

The dog-food failure is a modelling error, and AnyList has already solved it. AnyList separates **Quantity**, "the number of packages or the amount by weight to be purchased", from **Package Size**: "information about the package size (e.g. 15-oz can or 2 liter bottle) is best entered in the Package Size field" ([AnyList Help](https://help.anylist.com/articles/add-item-quantity/)). FamilyOS's single "Default unit" mixes those two ideas, so "31.1 lb" has nowhere to go and the label means nothing to a non-specialist. The mock's **"How it comes" (31.1 lb bag)** is AnyList's Package Size in family language, and it should be adopted as is. It belongs on the household product, because "the big bag" is stable over time. How many to buy belongs on the list entry, because it changes from trip to trip.

The heavier alternative is a trap. Grocy modelled purchase units, stock units and a conversion factor between them. In v4.0.0 (July 2023) it **removed the "Factor purchase to stock" option** in favour of general conversions ([Grocy changelog](https://grocy.info/changelog)). A 2026 hands-on found its product form had "too many fields" and was hard to get household members to use ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/grocy-seemed-like-a-great-self-hosted-tool-until-i-used-it-for-a-week/)). FamilyOS should store package size as **free text for display plus an optional parsed value and unit**. Use Instacart's vocabulary of lowercase abbreviations (oz, lb, g, kg, fl oz, ct, pack, doz) and its "6 x 12 fl oz" multipack format, which shoppers already recognize ([Instacart Docs](https://docs.instacart.com/ads_partners/product_content_guidelines/size)). The parsed value only pays off later, for unit-price comparison. Baymard found that **86% of e-commerce sites fail to show price per unit**, and shoppers get the mental math wrong ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/price-per-unit)). That is a reason to capture the number now, not a reason to build price comparison yet.

Adapt the mock's **"How many we usually get"** rather than adopting it as a form field. NN/g recommends smart defaults drawn from history ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/mobile-input-checklist/)), and FamilyOS already records purchases at trip completion. The usual count can be learned from that history and shown as an editable default rather than asked up front. On the list row, the quantity control should be a **horizontal [−] 1 [+] stepper with targets of at least 1 cm**, paired with a tappable number that opens a decimal keypad. NN/g recommends steppers exactly when one value (here, 1) dominates, and recommends pairing them with typed entry ([NN/g: Input steppers](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/input-steppers/)). Changing quantity directly on the row also addresses a recurring complaint about OurGroceries: you can't change a quantity without opening a detail screen ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id325851015)). Throughout, labels should use plain words: "How it comes", "How many", "Where we buy it". NN/g finds that even experts prefer plain language and advises checking that users actually know any jargon before using it ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/)).

## Learn the store's layout instead of asking for aisle numbers

The mock's **"Aisle" field is the part the evidence challenges most**. No mainstream app asks users to type aisle numbers. They remember a category for each item after one correction, and they order categories per store. Apple Reminders' Groceries list remembers a manual section change "next time you add that item" ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105086)). That learning matters because its automatic sorting sometimes goes wrong: frozen blueberries landed in Beverages, and iPhoneLife notes that a bad autosort "can do more harm than good" ([iPhoneLife](https://www.iphonelife.com/content/iphone-grocery-list-sorting-wrong-heres-how-to-fix-it)). AnyList's "category sets" give each store its own section order, and binding a set to a store filter reorders the list automatically ([AnyList Help](https://help.anylist.com/articles/category-sets/)). OurGroceries remembers a drag-rearranged order "for the next time that you create a shopping list" ([User Guide](https://www.ourgroceries.com/user-guide)). The "Grocery – Smart Shopping List" app goes furthest. It "keeps track of the order you mark off items to sort your list next time", and keeps a separate learned order for each store ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/grocery-smart-shopping-list/id1195676848)).

FamilyOS already groups by store, so it is one step from the best version of this. Keep a **section (category) on the household product**, guessed from a small seed map and overwritten whenever someone drags it. Then **learn a per-store section order from check-off timestamps** on each completed trip, with drag-to-reorder as a manual override. This removes a setup screen and answers two real complaints: OurGroceries users who shop at several stores want a store dimension, and Bring! users say "there are way more aisles in grocery stores than allowed here" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id325851015); [justuseapp Bring!](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/580669177/bring-shopping-list-recipes/reviews)). If the family wants a literal aisle number for a few awkward items, add it as an optional per-store note, not a primary field. "Where we buy it" should allow more than one store, as AnyList's store assignment does ([AnyList](https://help.anylist.com/articles/assign-items-to-stores/)), because staples like eggs are bought wherever the family happens to be.

## In-store mode is where families keep or abandon a list

A September 2026 comparison names the reasons families give up on a shared list: **only one person adds items, sync fails at checkout, items aren't organized by store section, or staples vanish after purchase** ([Homsy](https://gethomsy.com/blog/meal-planning/shared-grocery-list-app)). The sorting fix above addresses the third reason. The rest is about the shopping moment, and on the plus side real-time sync is the most-praised feature across reviews: "If my husband picked up batteries at one store, I see it right away" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/-/id325851015)).

Check-off should be **one tap on the whole row**, at least 48 px tall. Android requires 48 dp targets ([Android Developers](https://developer.android.com/guide/topics/ui/accessibility/apps)), WCAG 2.2 AA requires a minimum of 24 px and suggests 44 px for important controls ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html)), and NN/g suggests about 2 cm for children aged 3 to 8 ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/children-ux-physical-development/)). Checked items should sink into a collapsed **"In the cart" section**, as they do in OurGroceries and AnyList. Offer a "recent check-offs first" option for fixing mis-taps ([User Guide](https://www.ourgroceries.com/user-guide)). Avoid swipe-to-delete as the main gesture. Swipes are invisible and easy to trigger by accident ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/contextual-swipe/)), and Bring! users complain it is "so easy to unintentionally remove an item while just trying to edit" ([justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/580669177/bring-shopping-list-recipes/reviews)). Use an undo snackbar rather than "Are you sure?" prompts, which people learn to ignore ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/)). Keep a specific confirmation only for bulk clears such as "Remove 14 checked items?".

The platform now supports a proper shopping mode. **Screen Wake Lock is Baseline 2025** ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Screen_Wake_Lock_API)), and since **iOS 18.4** it also works in home-screen web apps ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/16574/webkit-features-in-safari-18-4/)). Request it on entering shopping mode and request it again on `visibilitychange`. iOS has **no Background Sync** ([firt.dev](https://firt.dev/notes/pwa-ios/)), so queue check-offs and adds in IndexedDB and flush them on `online`, `visibilitychange` and launch. Show a plain "Offline — will sync" banner and a "synced N s ago" indicator like OurGroceries' ([User Guide](https://www.ourgroceries.com/user-guide)). Hoober's field study found **49% of phone use is one-handed** ([UXmatters](https://www.uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2013/02/how-do-users-really-hold-mobile-devices.php)), so in shopping mode the add box belongs at the bottom of the screen. Haptics can't carry the confirmation on their own, because the Vibration API is unavailable on iOS.

Collaboration gets the biggest return from a feature FamilyOS has nearly built already. Bring! is the one app designed around telling others "you're going to the store" and notifying them of changes ([getbring.com](https://www.getbring.com/en/home)), and its reviews credit it with ending "No more Mom can you bring…" ([justuseapp](https://justuseapp.com/en/app/580669177/bring-shopping-list-recipes/reviews)). A **"Heading to Costco" broadcast** over the existing Web Push channel invites last-minute adds before the shopper leaves. AnyList keeps its change notifications optional ([AnyList](https://www.anylist.com/lists)), so per-item change pushes should be opt-in and batched.

"Complete trip" already puts FamilyOS ahead of most list apps, which simply delete crossed-off items. Paprika's split is the one to copy: purchased items move to the pantry, and **unpurchased items stay on the list automatically** ([Paprika](https://www.paprikaapp.com/help/ios/)). That carry-over directly answers the "staples vanish after purchase" reason for giving up on a list.

## Keep the pantry light and treat barcodes as household memory

The proposed Pantry page holds up if it stays a **catalog-hygiene page, not an inventory ledger**. Grocy users report that logging every item used is unrealistic: one rushed dinner and the inventory is "completely out of whack" ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/grocy-seemed-like-a-great-self-hosted-tool-until-i-used-it-for-a-week/)). One long-term Grocy user still bridged its list to Bring! because Bring! worked better in the store ([heig.net](https://www.heig.net/home/20220525-grocy)). The mainstream apps rely on recents and favorites; AnyList users give favorites "a quick run through" before shopping ([AnyList](https://help.anylist.com/articles/weekly-shopping/)). FamilyOS's interval-based "probably need soon" chips already beat that without logging consumption. Add at most an optional "running low" toggle or minimum-stock setting for a handful of staples such as paper towels and eggs. The **"needs details" queue** is the right companion to silent catalog growth: it lets a parent fill in "How it comes" and the section at leisure, never at the moment of entry. **Merge duplicates** is a differentiator. No researched app documents a user-facing merge or alias tool, and synonym handling is weak across the category. Make merging a deliberate parent action ("Merge 'TP' into 'Toilet paper'") that moves list history and barcodes to the surviving product, and keep real variants (2% vs whole milk) as separate products.

For barcodes, what matters most is the not-found path. OurGroceries shows "unknown barcode", lets the user type a name, and **remembers that name for future scans** ([User Guide](https://www.ourgroceries.com/user-guide)). Grocy's Android app shows only "unknown" with no Open Food Facts suggestion, and a user filed an issue about it ([GitHub #968](https://github.com/patzly/grocy-android/issues/968)). The mock's merged search/scan box should make scan an inline action, and a miss should drop straight into the tap-to-fill sheet with the barcode attached. On the data side, Open Food Facts' universal endpoint `/api/v3/product/{barcode}?product_type=all` routes to food, **pet food**, beauty or general products ([OFF tutorial](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/documentation/docs/Product-Opener/api/tutorials/scanning-cosmetics-pet-food-and-other-products/)), which matters for the dog food. Reads are limited to **15 per minute per IP**, apps must send a custom User-Agent, and the data is ODbL-licensed with CC-BY-SA images ([OFF API docs](https://openfoodfacts.github.io/openfoodfacts-server/api/)). US coverage is roughly **0.8 million products** ([OFF United States](https://world.openfoodfacts.org/country/en:united-states)), so expect regular misses on household goods. OFF's `quantity` field holds the size exactly as printed ("400g", "6", "2 x 160 g"), and `product_quantity` normalizes it to grams or ml ([OFF data fields](https://static.openfoodfacts.org/data/data-fields.txt)). Prefill "How it comes" from the raw string, so it shows "31.1 lb" rather than 14,107 g. Route lookups through the server so family IPs stay private and caching and rate limiting live in one place. Normalize barcodes to GTIN-14 (expand UPC-E, left-pad, validate the check digit) ([Wikipedia: GTIN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Trade_Item_Number)). Use the UPCitemdb free trial, at 100 requests a day ([UPCitemdb](https://devs.upcitemdb.com/)), only as a fallback. Show an "Open Food Facts (ODbL)" credit wherever OFF data appears.

Kids need their own lightweight path. None of the apps researched offers a request or approval flow; household sharing is all-or-nothing. Bring!'s picture tiles are praised as intuitive, and its "Urgent" and "If convenient" priority tags work as soft requests ([MakeUseOf](https://www.makeuseof.com/organize-family-shopping-with-bring-grocery-app/)). FamilyOS's existing "who's it for" tag can grow into an **"asked for" state**: a kid's add appears in a distinct sticker style that a parent approves with one tap or downgrades to "if convenient". Pair it with picture or emoji tiles for common items, which suit pre-readers and the Sticker Book look. This part is a design inference. No study tests it, so try it with the kids before building out permissions.

## Conclusion

The research changes the framing more than the feature list. FamilyOS's real problem isn't a missing "package size" field. It is that the product catalog is something the family must deliberately maintain, while the best apps make the catalog a by-product of using the list. Once every typed word becomes a product, the rest of the mock follows: "needs details" becomes a natural inbox, merging becomes necessary hygiene, barcodes attach to something, sections and store order can be learned, and trip history can fill in the usual quantity. The design rule is to collect detail at the moment of least effort: at the shelf through scans and check-off order, at checkout through trip completion, and at a quiet moment through the Pantry inbox. Never collect it at the moment of adding. Two openings that none of the commercial apps offer suit a private family tool: a parent-approved kid request flow, and a merge tool that lets the household's own vocabulary ("TP", "the big dog food") become the canonical names.
